    A five-minute walkthrough · one real comparison

    Two ideas walk in. One policy decides.

    PairwisePM is a mechanical judge for product managers choosing between two competing Generative AI ideas. Both ideas enter on one questionnaire, on one screen, in one sitting. Out comes a winner, a win probability with an interval, and the exact levers that would flip the verdict.

    There is no LLM at runtime. The runtime is weights and a bias term — the LLM's job ended at build time. The model applies your own stated policy more consistently than you can. It removes the Tuesdays.

    runs fully offline no API key your log stays in your hands numpy, not neural

    In the next six stops you will follow one seeded comparison end to end: a PM on an established support product weighing A · Reply Drafts against B · Instant Summaries in 1→N (allocation) mode. Every number you'll see is computed by the model exactly as specified — nothing is mocked up.

    Stop 1 · Why this exists

    Same PM. Different Tuesday. Different call.

    Here is a real week in the life of a prioritization spreadsheet. One PM, the same two GenAI ideas, scored twice with RICE — three days apart.

    • 1Occasion noise. Nothing about the ideas changed between Monday and Thursday. The judge did — mood, anchors, which demo they saw last. The same PM quietly disagrees with themselves.
    • 2False precision. 47.3 versus 44.1 looks like a measurement. It's four soft 1–10 guesses multiplied together. The decimal places dress noise up as signal.
    • 3The verdict flips. Thursday's spreadsheet reverses Monday's roadmap. Add a second PM and the disagreement gets worse, not better.

    hover a note to spotlight it in the screen →

    The fix is not better guessing. It's a structure where noise cancels: score both ideas together, in one sitting, and judge only the difference. Whatever mood you're in, you're in it for both ideas — the pairwise delta subtracts it out.

    Stop 2 · The input

    Both ideas. One screen. One sitting.

    The whole interview fits on one screen, by design — splitting it across sessions would let occasion noise back in. Seven factors in 1→N mode, each with an anchored scale, a stated prior weight, and a written rationale.

    • 1Two modes, different factor sets. 0→1 for PMF search, 1→N for roadmap allocation. This comparison runs in 1→N.
    • 2Anchored scales, not 1–10 vibes. Impact is scored against a named past launch. Feasibility is your eval's pass rate, not optimism.
    • 3Every factor is tagged lever or fact. A lever can be moved by working; a fact is what it is. The leverage panel at Stop 4 runs on these tags.
    • 4Strategic fit never touches the score. Evaluative judgments are captured and displayed as labeled override inputs — excluded from the mechanical arithmetic, on purpose.
    • 5The weights are a policy, not a fit. They ship as editable config with a rationale per factor. You can read them, argue with them, change them.

    Stop 3 · The output

    A probability with an interval — never a 47.3

    The judge takes the difference between the two z-scored factor vectors, applies the stated weights, and passes it through a logistic. What comes out is a claim sized to what the model actually knows.

    • 159%, not "A wins." Read it as: your stated policy favors A about three times in five. That is a claim about consistency with your policy — not a prediction that A will succeed in the market.
    • 2The interval is always shown. A bootstrap over the seven factor contributions puts this verdict at 52–64%. If that band ever straddles 50%, no winner is declared — you'll see that at Stop 5.
    • 3M0 badge: this model trains on nothing. It is your stated prior weights applied mechanically. After 10 logged decisions in a mode, M1 starts learning your revealed weights from your own picks.
    • 4The whole model fits in one line. Auditable in a glance. An equal-weight ablation toggle is one click away — the scientific control that famously performs near the optimum.

    Stop 4 · The actionable part

    Not just who won — what would flip it

    A verdict you can't act on is trivia. Because the score is linear, every factor's exact contribution is visible, and the smallest change that would reach parity is computable in raw units — no statistics degree required.

    • 1Where the margin comes from. A wins on impact, unit economics, feasibility and flywheel; B claws back a little on reach and risk. Effort contributes nothing — both are two-sprint builds, so the delta cancels.
    • 2Path to parity, in B's own units. The cheapest single-factor route to 50/50 runs through impact — and the panel is honest that it sits near the top of the scale. There is no single-lever route to 60/40.
    • 3Levers only. Reach and flywheel are tagged facts, so no parity path is proposed through them — the tool never advises you to wish a fact were different. If every path ran through facts, it would say so: "loses on fundamentals."
    • 4Fragility check. The panel probes whether one small, plausible re-estimate (up to 0.75σ on a single factor) flips the winner. Here the smallest verdict-flipping move is far larger — so the flag stays quiet. When it wouldn't be, it says: re-estimate before committing.

    Fragility check

    Not fragile. The smallest single-factor move that flips this verdict is 1.38σ on impact — far above the 0.75σ "small plausible move" threshold, so the flag stays quiet. The interval, not this flag, is what narrows first — see the next stop.

    Stop 5 · The part that makes it trustworthy

    A judge that knows when to shut up

    A judge that overstates its confidence is worse than no judge — PMs learn to ignore it, or worse, obey it. Three behaviors are built in as construction, not policy.

    • 1The verdict is withheld, mechanically. Remember Stop 4's advice to re-estimate B's feasibility? Suppose the summary eval is re-run with name-handling fixed and B's pass rate rises to 3.3. The margin shrinks to σ(0.185) = 55% — and now the 90% interval, 47–62%, straddles the 50% line.
    • 2It tells you what to do instead. Not "pick anyway" — gather evidence, and the leverage panel already told you which evidence moves the needle most.

    • 10→1 mode never claims predictive accuracy. Searching for product–market fit is a domain where nobody's model predicts outcomes — so this one refuses to pretend. The banner is permanent, not dismissible.
    • 2The ceiling flag routes around the math — and the card says why. A potential outlier idea is precisely the thing a weighted average destroys, and the verdict card explains that in its own words. Top-box ceiling never enters the score — it routes the idea to human portfolio judgment instead.

    Stop 6 · The offer

    Small tool. Hard spine.

    What you get

    • A Hugging Face Gradio Space — and the same repo pip installs locally.
    • Fully offline runtime — in the app's own words: "Runs entirely in this app. No API key, no server, nothing stored anywhere but your own log file." (numpy, no LLM, no network calls.)
    • Your decision log as downloadable JSONL — nothing stored server-side, ever. The log is yours and it is the training set.
    • Editable factor configs with written rationales — the policy is yours to argue with.
    • Designed so a full A-vs-B comparison, blank form to verdict card, lands in under five minutes.

    What it refuses — on purpose

    • No LLM in the runtime loop.
    • No ranking of three-plus ideas; v1 is strictly A-vs-B.
    • No accuracy claims in 0→1 mode. In 1→N, accuracy is earned through calibration on your log — never asserted up front.
    • No strategic-fit or brand terms inside the mechanical score — displayed, labeled, excluded.
    • No forced winner when the interval straddles 50%.
